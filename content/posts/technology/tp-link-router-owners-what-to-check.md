If you have a TP-Link router, the recent headlines can sound as if you should throw it away. You don't have to. The FCC, in [its own guidance on the new rules](https://www.fcc.gov/faqs-recent-updates-fcc-covered-list-regarding-routers-produced-foreign-countries), says consumers using these routers at home "do not need to do anything".

There is a real issue underneath, though, and it applies to every brand: a router that no longer receives security updates is a risk. We have not tested any TP-Link hardware. This guide is drawn from the FCC, TP-Link's own lists and published reporting on the lawsuits.

## What the Lawsuits Actually Say

Florida, Iowa, Montana and Nebraska [sued TP-Link Systems on 6 October](https://thehackernews.com/2026/10/tp-link-sued-by-four-more-us-states.html). Texas had already [filed in February](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-tp-link-router-lawsuits-fcc-ban-us/). These are consumer-protection cases, and nothing has been decided in court. The states allege that TP-Link overstated how secure its routers are and how separate it is from China.

Three details are easy to lose in the coverage:

- **Three of the complaints allege no built-in backdoor.** According to The Hacker News, which read them, those from Florida, Montana and Nebraska do not say TP-Link built one, or that the Chinese government has obtained customers' data through the company. They describe that as a risk under Chinese law. Iowa's announcement is worded more strongly in places.
- **TP-Link denies the claims.** Its corporate affairs officer called the suits "built on false premises", and the company says it does not share customer network data with foreign governments.
- **The lawsuits do not stop you using your router.** As TechRepublic puts it, they "do not prohibit Americans from using routers they already own."

## What the FCC Rule Does

Since 23 March, new models of foreign-made consumer routers cannot get the FCC authorisation they need to go on sale, unless they win a conditional approval. The rule goes by where a router is made, not who makes it, so it is not specific to TP-Link.

For owners, the FCC's answers are short:

- Are there restrictions on using these routers? "No."
- Can you still buy models that were already authorised? Yes.
- Can they still get firmware updates? Yes, under a waiver that [runs until at least 1 March 2027](https://thehackernews.com/2026/10/tp-link-sued-by-four-more-us-states.html).

That date is worth noting. It is a floor, and we do not know what happens after it.

## The Risk That Applies to You: Old Firmware

The complaints set TP-Link's marketing against routers that were hacked and models that no longer get fixes. In April the FBI said Russian military intelligence had compromised TP-Link routers through a flaw called CVE-2023-50224, changing their DNS settings to collect passwords and login tokens. TP-Link said that, with one exception, the affected products had reached end of life.

The complaints also point to two versions of the Archer AX21, which they say reached end of life in May 2024 and no longer get fixes.

## Five Things to Check

**1. Find your exact model and hardware version.** Both are on the label under the router. The version matters: one model can have several, with different support dates.

**2. Look it up on TP-Link's end-of-life list.** TP-Link publishes a [US list for home products](https://static.tp-link.com/upload/manual/2026/202604/20260422/US%20EOL_List_Home%20-%20V8.pdf). The copy we found is dated April 2026, so a newer one may exist. It is also worth looking at your model's support page for the date of the latest firmware.

**3. If it is supported, update it.** Check for firmware in the router's app or admin page, and turn on automatic updates if the model offers them.

**4. Check the settings an attacker would change.** Confirm the DNS servers are the ones you expect, give the admin login a long unique password, and turn off remote management unless you use it. This is general good practice rather than advice specific to TP-Link.

**5. If your internet provider supplied the box, ask them.** TP-Link's Aginet line goes out through ISPs. Five flaws in those devices were disclosed in August, and the fixes reach customers through the provider, not from TP-Link directly.

## If Your Model Is No Longer Supported

Replace it, whichever brand it is. Nothing here calls for a panic purchase, and previously authorised models from many makers are still on sale. Before buying, check how long the maker promises updates and whether the router installs them by itself.

If your router is on the supported list and up to date, there is nothing further the rules or the lawsuits ask you to do. Check again in the spring, when the firmware waiver's date gets closer.
