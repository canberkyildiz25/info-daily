**Updated 19 October 2026.** An earlier version of this guide priced 32GB of DDR5 at about $70 and a 1TB SSD at about $60, for a $730 build. Those prices were already wrong when it was published: in February, [PCWorld paid $312.99](https://www.pcworld.com/article/3050047/how-to-build-a-1000-gaming-pc-in-2026-dont.html) for a 32GB DDR5 kit. We have rewritten the guide around current prices.

The short answer is no, not with new parts. [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/best-picks/best-pc-builds-gaming), which maintains a set of recommended builds, dropped its cheapest tier this year, saying "$500 just won't cut it at today's prices." Its least expensive build now costs $924, and that is before any memory.

## Where the Money Goes Now

This is Tom's Hardware's cheapest recommended build, as priced on 14 October:

- **Processor:** Intel Core Ultra 250K Plus, $219
- **Motherboard:** ASRock B860 Rock Wifi 7, $139
- **Graphics card:** AMD Radeon RX 7600, $339
- **Memory:** 16GB DDR5-6000, $258
- **Storage:** 512GB PCIe 3.0 SSD, $82
- **Case:** $60
- **Power supply:** 650W, $60
- **Cooler:** $25

Without the memory, that comes to **$924**. Tom's Hardware leaves RAM out of its totals because the price moves so much, and because bundle deals can lower it. Add the $258 kit and the real figure is **$1,182**.

Compare it with what this guide used to say. The same class of graphics card is about $100 dearer: Tom's Hardware notes the RX 7600 was $240 the last time it priced the build. The memory is half the capacity at more than three times the price. The SSD is half the size. None of this is unusual. It is the [memory shortage](/technology/ram-and-ssd-prices-which-pc-upgrades-still-make-sense) reaching every part that contains memory chips, graphics cards included.

That build is still a capable 1080p gaming PC. Tom's Hardware says the RX 7600 averages well over 60fps at 1080p on ultra settings. The weak point is 16GB of RAM, which may mean closing other programs during demanding games.

## Three Ways to Spend Less

**Use DDR4 instead of DDR5.** Older platforms take DDR4 memory, which costs less right now. PCWorld's second attempt at a budget build used an AMD Ryzen 5500X, an A520 motherboard and DDR4. It came to $1,003.62 including tax and shipping. Tom's Hardware also argues a DDR4 build is [worth considering](https://www.tomshardware.com/desktops/pc-building/examining-ddr4-pc-build-options-in-2026-with-high-ddr5-prices-a-pc-build-with-ddr4-is-worth-considering-especially-if-you-have-ram-you-can-carry-over-from-a-previous-build), especially if you have DDR4 from an old PC to carry over. The trade-off is an older platform with fewer upgrades ahead of it.

**Reuse what you already have.** A case, a decent power supply and an SSD from an older PC all carry over to a new build. So does DDR4 memory, if you go the DDR4 route. Each one is money you don't spend at today's prices.

**Consider a used graphics card.** It is the most expensive single part, and the one where the second-hand market saves the most. Buy from a seller who lets you see the card running, and check what warranty remains.

## When a Prebuilt Is the Better Deal

This used to be the expensive option. It may not be right now. PCWorld's verdict in February was that this is "one of the very few market conditions in which a pre-built PC makes sense", even for people who can build their own. It found a discounted Dell desktop at Micro Center for $999.99, with 32GB of DDR5 and an RTX 5060 Ti. Tom's Hardware has made [the same observation](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/ddr5/32gb-of-ddr5-6000-ram-now-costs-usd450-ai-induced-shortage-reaches-worst-milestone-yet) this month: price rises are reaching prebuilt systems more slowly than loose parts.

Before you order parts, price a prebuilt with the same graphics card and memory. Include Windows, which a self-built PC needs and a prebuilt already has.

## Or Don't Buy a PC This Year

If the goal is simply to play at 1080p for the least money, a console is now cheaper. The [Switch 2 is $499 and a PS5 Digital Edition $599](/gaming/consoles-have-never-cost-more-make-yours-last), each roughly half the cost of the build above. And if you already own a PC that is merely slow, upgrading the one part that holds it back costs far less than replacing it.

## Plan for About $1,200, or Change the Plan

A new budget gaming PC with current parts costs roughly $1,200 this autumn, not $700. That is still a good machine. If $1,200 is too much, the realistic options are a DDR4 build that reuses old parts, a prebuilt bought on sale, or a console.
