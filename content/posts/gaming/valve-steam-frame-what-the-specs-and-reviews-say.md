Valve released the Steam Frame on 18 September 2026 with no real warning, at $1,059. It is a standalone wireless headset that runs SteamOS on an ARM chip, and its central idea is not native VR at all — it is streaming your existing PC library to your face without a cable.

**We have not tested this headset.** Everything below is either from Valve's own published specification or from reviewers who have used the device, and each claim says which. The useful thing about the Steam Frame's launch is that the reviews agree with each other to an unusual degree — on what is good and on what is not.

## What Valve Confirms

From [the Steam Frame store page](https://store.steampowered.com/steamframe):

- **Price:** $1,059 for 256GB, $1,299 for 1TB. Each kit ships with the Steam Frame Controllers, a Wi-Fi 6E adapter for PC streaming, and a copy of *Half-Life: Alyx*.
- **Displays:** 2160 × 2160 LCD per eye, at 72, 80, 90, 120 or 144Hz.
- **Chip:** Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Gen 3.
- **Memory:** 16GB LPDDR5X, with a microSD slot alongside the built-in storage.
- **Operating system:** SteamOS — the same Arch Linux-based system as the Steam Deck, not Android.
- **Battery:** 21.6Wh.
- **Weight:** 440g complete with headstrap and facial interface; the core module alone is 185g.

Note what that battery figure does *not* tell you: how long it runs. Valve publishes the capacity, not the runtime. Reviewers measured the runtime, and it is the story of this launch.

## Where Every Review Agrees

Launch reviews for hardware usually diverge. These did not.

**It is the most comfortable headset they have worn.** This is close to unanimous. The reason is structural rather than about padding: Valve put the battery at the *back* of the strap, so it acts as a counterweight to the module over your eyes instead of adding to it. [UploadVR's hands-on](https://www.uploadvr.com/valve-steam-frame-hands-on-impressions/) describes it as significantly lighter-feeling than any other fully-featured standalone headset, and the 185g front module is the number that explains why.

**Streaming is the thing it does well.** [Tom's Hardware's review](https://www.tomshardware.com/virtual-reality/valve-steam-frame-review) is direct about the balance: competent as standalone VR, but wireless streaming from a PC remains the focus, and the bundled Wi-Fi 6E adapter exists because Valve knows it.

**The battery is the problem.** This is the sharpest and most repeated criticism. Reviewers report roughly **90 minutes** in demanding games, and around **one to one and a half hours** of mixed use — with demanding standalone play approaching a single hour. [PC Gamer, after three weeks with it](https://www.pcgamer.com/hardware/vr-hardware/valve-steam-frame-review/), does not soften this. A headset you have to plan a session around is a different product from one you pick up.

**The software is unfinished.** Desktop and multi-window use was described across reviews as unstable, with crashes. Game compatibility when running standalone on the ARM chip is inconsistent, and the list of verified titles at launch is short.

**It arrived late.** [Engadget's verdict](https://www.engadget.com/2257659/valve-steam-frame-review/) — "solid standalone VR that's too little, too late" — is the one reviewers keep circling. The headset is good; the category moved while Valve was building it.

Smaller complaints that came up repeatedly: the on-board speakers are poor, and non-VR gaming is limited by what an ARM processor can do with a library built for x86.

## What This Means If You're Buying

The Steam Frame is not competing on price and is not pretending to. At $1,059 it sits well above a Quest, and reviewers consistently name the price as painful rather than justified.

It makes sense if:

- You already own a gaming PC strong enough to stream from, and the cable is the only reason you stopped using VR.
- You play in sessions of an hour or so, at a desk, near a charger.
- Comfort is the thing that has previously made you put a headset down.

It does not make sense if:

- You want a standalone headset to use away from a PC. The battery and the native library both argue against it.
- You expect to play long sessions untethered.
- You want a finished software experience at launch. Give it a few updates.

## The Short Version

Valve built the most comfortable headset on the market and attached a battery that runs for about ninety minutes. Reviewers are consistent about both halves of that sentence, which is unusual and makes the buying decision clearer than most launches do: if you will use it within arm's reach of a charger and a strong PC, the complaints mostly do not apply to you. If you will not, they all do.
